Two terms come up all the time in immersion learning: "comprehensible input" and "i+1". Both answer the same question: **what should I watch or read to actually learn something?** Content that's too easy teaches you nothing new because you already understand all of it; content that's too hard teaches you nothing either, because you can't follow a single line. This page explains the idea itself and then makes it practical — how to choose a show or book at the right difficulty.

## What does comprehensible input mean?

The term comes from linguist Stephen Krashen's "input hypothesis". The core idea is simple:

> "We acquire language in only one way: by understanding messages."

In other words, a language isn't "learned" by memorising rules — it's "acquired" in the process of **understanding messages**. As a child you never memorised your native language's grammar tables, yet you speak it more naturally than any grammar book; what got you there was a massive amount of input you mostly understood.

The key word is "comprehensible". A foreign-language film you don't understand at all teaches you very little even after a hundred viewings: you can't tell what's being said, so your brain has nothing to connect. But when you understand most of a sentence and only one or two bits are new, context, picture and tone help you guess those bits — and that guess is where acquisition happens.

## What is i+1?

"i" is your current level, "+1" is a little above it. **i+1 input is input you can mostly understand at your current level, with a small amount of new material in it.**

To be clear: this is a way of thinking about choosing material, not a formula you can measure. Nobody can calculate what your "i" is right now, and no show is exactly "+1". In practice, treat it as a direction: **pick content you can keep watching, but don't fully understand.**

For mining there's an especially handy version of i+1: **the sentence with exactly one unknown word**. You can already read everything in it except that word, and the context explains the word for you — the best possible card material. Why cards should carry the original sentence is covered in [What is sentence mining?](/faq/sentence-mining.en)

## How do I tell if a show or book is the right difficulty?

There's no precise test, but there are useful signals:

**Probably too hard:**

- Without helper subtitles, you have no idea what happened in an episode;
- every line needs several lookups, and you still don't get it afterwards;
- after ten minutes you feel tired and want to watch something else;
- you notice you're "studying" rather than "watching".

**Probably about right:**

- You follow the general plot, and some of the lines you hear or read make sense;
- there are plenty of unknown words, but you can keep watching without looking them up, and lookups make things clear;
- when an episode ends, you want the next one.

**Probably too easy:**

- You understand nearly every line and rarely meet a word worth looking up;
- it's still great input (for listening and speed), but you can start moving to harder content.

The most important factor isn't actually difficulty — it's **interest**. A slightly-too-hard show you really want to watch often works better than a perfectly pitched one you don't care about, because you'll keep watching. As the immersion guide says: choosing content you like matters more than anything. You can lower difficulty with helpers; you can't manufacture interest.

## How does content roughly rank from easy to hard?

These are general tendencies, not rules — works within one genre vary a lot:

- **Rewatching something you've seen in your own language**: you know the plot, so it's naturally a notch easier — an excellent choice when starting out.
- **Slice-of-life, school and everyday shows**: the dialogue is mostly everyday conversation with common vocabulary and situations.
- **Anime for young children**: slower and simpler, though not necessarily to your taste.
- **Battle, fantasy, sci-fi, mystery and historical works**: lots of specialised vocabulary (techniques, world-building, jargon, archaic language), usually harder.
- **For reading**: manga gets help from the pictures and is a good first step; light novels are generally easier than literary fiction; voiced visual novels train listening and reading together.

You can also adjust the difficulty of the same work by how you watch it: Japanese subtitles are easier than none, and a blurred native-language secondary subtitle catches you when you're stuck. Stage-by-stage advice on subtitles is in [Japanese or native-language subtitles?](/faq/subtitle-language.en)

## How do I use comprehensible input with Fushi?

What Fushi does is pull content that's a bit too hard for you back into the comprehensible range:

- **Tap to look up**: words in subtitles, novel text and manga (via OCR) can be tapped for their definitions, turning "that one word I didn't get" into one you do. If you're looking things up constantly, every line, that's a sign the content is on the hard side.
- **Use frequency to decide what's worth keeping**: with frequency dictionaries installed (the [recommended pack](/faq/pack.en) in onboarding includes Japanese ones), the lookup popup shows each word's frequency. Common words are worth a card; very rare ones can wait — they'll turn up again in a better place. More on reading frequencies in [What is word frequency?](/faq/frequency.en)
- **Mine only i+1 sentences**: on a sentence that's "just this one word short", tap the plus to make a card with the sentence, the scene and the original audio; skip sentences you don't understand at all.
- **Reorder new cards by frequency**: "Reorder new cards by frequency" in **Settings → Card creation** puts common words first (AnkiConnect only), so your "i" grows first across the most frequently used words.
- **Tune difficulty with a secondary subtitle**: put your native language in the secondary subtitle and blur it, glancing only when completely stuck — see [How do I use a secondary subtitle as a bridge?](/faq/secondary-subtitle.en)
- **Audiobook plus text**: a book you struggle to read becomes easier when you listen to its audiobook while following the text, compared with reading or listening alone — see [Audiobooks](/faq/audiobook.en).

## Common misconceptions

- **"I'll wait until my vocabulary is big enough for real content"**: however much you prepare, you won't understand everything the first time you meet real content. Comprehensible input doesn't require full understanding — mostly is enough. You can start once you're partway through a basic deck.
- **"Harder means faster progress"**: input too hard to understand gives the slowest progress of all, and is the most likely to make you quit.
- **"I have to understand every word"**: you don't. For many words a rough guess from context is enough, and they get clearer the next time. Tolerating ambiguity is one of the skills immersion trains most.
- **"Comprehensible input means only listening, no studying"**: lots of input is the main track, but active tools — lookups, basic vocabulary, mining cards — make more content comprehensible sooner. The two work together.
